# The mystery test: research and why our two best stories work

Updated: 2026-11-10 (writers' room, `claude/writers-mystery`)

The owner's notes this came from, 2026-11-10:
- "some of the pacing is a little bit slow. Also, there's no like mystery. There's no like question to the user... the
  mystery is like what draws them in to keep them watching"
- "need to cut right when the reveal"
- "the best one I think is the carpet one" (Light Sleeper)
- "there's a delicate balance between being like too obvious and being confusing"

This page has three parts: what works online (with links), why Light Sleeper and Runaway work, and the rules we take
from both. STANDARDS 1.0 (the mystery test) is the rule. This page gives the reasons behind it.

**The genre's pattern.** The leading Roblox story template is "In This Town / In This World, [a strange rule]...". The
rule is the question: the viewer wants to know why it exists, and the reveal is the hidden reason. The top performer
(7.45M, 26 s) is the tightest: one question, two clues that read two ways, the reveal at 77%, one beat, out.
Light Sleeper's "My village is covered in nine thousand carpets" is exactly this shape. Our job is to make every story
that shape.

### Principles, with sources
- **Curiosity is a gap you feel you can close.** Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994) says curiosity needs some
  prior knowledge, and it is strongest when you feel close to the answer. Kang et al. (2009) found curiosity peaks at
  *moderate* confidence. That is the owner's "between too obvious and confusing", measured.
  Sources: [Loewenstein, The Psychology of Curiosity](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf),
  [Kang et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/),
  [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/curiosity-depends-on-what-you-already-know-235803),
  [NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/).
  For us, the prior knowledge is **Minecraft's rules**. A player who knows the rule feels the answer is reachable.
- **Surprising yet inevitable.** Aristotle's *Poetics* says the effect is best when events come "by surprise" and
  "follow as cause and effect" ([text](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/aristotle/poetics.asp?pg=12),
  [gloss](https://english.hawaii.edu/criticalink/aristotle/gloss/gloss11.html)). A vanilla mechanic is our "necessity".
- **Fair play.** Knox's rules say the solution never rests on clues hidden from the reader
  ([Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Knox), [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/ronald-knox-detective-fiction-commandments)).
  Chekhov: plant what fires, fire what's planted ([Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun)).
- **Hide clues in the picture.** *The Sixth Sense* codes red around the supernatural. Viewers register it without
  decoding it, and it reads on a rewatch
  ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/the-sixth-sense-red-symbolism),
  [Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/articles/33625-the-sixth-sense-surprise-ending-is-obvious-if-you-pay-attention-to-these-6-clues)).
  Our carpets, the wool lid and the golem trailing the creeper do the same job.
- **Two-sentence horror.** A plain setup, then a second line that makes you reread the first: unexpected but logically
  consistent ([guide](https://dtadams.squarespace.com/blog/how-to-write-two-sentence-horror-stories)).
- **The first seconds.** YouTube Studio reports "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts
  ([help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)). YouTube publishes no benchmarks. Figures like "70-80 %
  viewed" or "half the drop-off is in the first 3 s" come from blogs and are unverified
  ([Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-hook-formulas),
  [Bytecap](https://www.bytecap.io/research/youtube-shorts-retention-benchmarks)). Use our own Shorts as the baseline.
- **Loop endings.** A last line that runs into the first, or repeats the hook's key word, makes the replay feel
  seamless ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/double-youtube-watch-time-looping-shorts),
  [ShortsFast](https://shortsfast.com/blog/write-a-loop-ending-for-youtube-shorts/)). One blog says every replay counts
  as a view; that wasn't confirmed in YouTube's own docs.

## 2. Why Light Sleeper works (the owner's best)

Line 1: "My village is covered in NINE THOUSAND CARPETS." The image is a striped carpet street into a carpeted plaza,
with a white-lidded well and a wool-boxed bell.

| part | what it is | why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The question (0-3 s)** | "why tf is the whole village covered in carpet?" | It's the "In This Town, [rule]" shape. It's one picture, it has a number, and it breaks a rule every player knows: nobody carpets a road. The question only the ending can answer is *what is the carpet for*. |
| **The plant** | Mom: "Walk SOFT, sweetie. The ground's a light SLEEPER." | It's literally true, but it's heard as a saying. A character who knows the truth says it plainly, and the narrator files it as mom-talk. On a rewatch it's the whole story in one line. Its job is to point the viewer **down** (the ground) without saying "monster". |
| **The clue** | Carpets everywhere, 300 sheep sheared every morning, the bell boxed in wool, the well's wool lid | It's a **picture clue repeated in every shot**: the viewer can't miss it, and can't read it until the rule clicks. Wool and carpet are vanilla's one "silent" material (no vibrations). A player gets it from the *pattern*: everything that could make noise is wrapped. |
| **The bomb under the table** | "Once I pulled the WOOL off the WELL. The bottom of the well SCREAMED." "She said it was a FOX." | A player knows that sound (the shrieker) here, at ~44%. A non-player knows *something* is down there. Both can guess, neither can be sure: **exactly the owner's balance**. "A fox" is a misread that passes (foxes do scream). |
| **The want** | A music disc; "Nobody here has ever heard a SONG." | A sympathetic want that *is* the danger. The viewer wants the song and dreads it. |
| **The reveal** | The warden rises out of the square; the sonic boom; the death message | The game says it, with no line explaining it. It's one step: noise wakes the thing under the ground. It changes line 1: the carpets weren't quirky, they were survival. |
| **What the owner didn't like** | "the delivery is off"; the cut | The hushed reading and the tail after the reveal. The story is the bar; the execution isn't. **Cut on the warden.** |

**Precisely why it works:** one question set by a picture; one plant that's literally true; one clue the picture
repeats until it's a pattern; one bomb-under-the-table moment where players get ahead; a want that walks into the
danger; and a reveal that is the game's own rule, shown.

## 3. Why Runaway works (the other bar)

Line 1: "All FOURTEEN villagers in my village RUN when they see ME."

| part | what it is | why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The question (0-3 s)** | "why tf does everyone run from him?" | Personal, countable, and it breaks a known rule: villagers ignore players. The question is about *the narrator*, so the answer is about him. |
| **The clues, misread** | the door slam; the farmer sprints; the fisherman never comes back; "Even the SHEEP pack up"; "Only the NITWIT stays" | **Escalation by distance.** Each is a joke *and* a clue (what you do when a creeper walks up). Nobody comments on them correctly, so they pass. |
| **The plant** | Mom: "I'm just SHY. She's the ONLY one who HUGS me." | It's literally true and misread as love. She can hug him because she's a creeper too. It's the same device as "the ground's a light sleeper". |
| **The picture clue** | The iron golem "follows me EVERYWHERE. I think he LIKES me." | Seen on screen and misread as affection. A golem watches threats. |
| **The bomb under the table** | "When I get EXCITED, I hear this HISSING." | At ~65% every player knows. From here it's dread, not mystery. That's Hitchcock's bomb: the viewer is ahead of the narrator. |
| **The want and the reveal** | the party; "So I invited MYSELF"; the blast and "blown up by Creeper" | The game does the reveal, with no "I was the creeper". It's one step, and it recontextualises every line before it. The POV camera *is* the twist: we never saw him. |

**Precisely why it works:** the question is about the narrator, so the reveal is about him; every clue is a laugh first
and a clue second; a character's loving line is the truest clue; and the camera device hides the answer in plain sight.

## 4. What both have, as rules (the mystery test, worked out)

1. **Line 1 is a strange fact the picture shows** ("In this town...", "My village...", "All fourteen..."), with a
   number. The viewer's *why* is set by 3 s. Never a mood, never an answer ("born with a rare condition").
2. **One character line that is literally true and heard as something else** (a saying, love, a joke). It's the best
   clue on a rewatch. Keep it to six words or fewer, in its own voice.
3. **One picture clue repeated until it's a pattern** (carpets; villagers fleeing). The viewer sees it every shot and
   can't read it until the rule clicks.
4. **A bomb under the table at 45-70 %**: the moment a Minecraft player can guess and a non-player knows *something's*
   there. Both guess, neither is sure. That's the owner's balance, and Kang's moderate confidence.
5. **A want that walks into it**: the narrator chooses (no accident), and what he wants is the danger.
6. **The game says the answer**: a mob, a death message, a chat line, a mechanic visibly working. No line explains it.
7. **Cut on it**: within ~1 s of the reveal frame. No "that's why", no sting, no epilogue. (Bloxolox's 15 s tail and
   Light Sleeper's tail are the anti-pattern.)
8. **One step back to line 1**: after the cut a viewer says one sentence ("the carpet kept the warden asleep", "he was
   the creeper"), and line 1 now means something else.
9. **25-40 s, nothing that doesn't move the question.** The 7.45M Short is 26 s.
10. **Don't give the twist away in the title or caption.** Name the rule or the question, never the secret.

The cold-reader check for 4 and 8 is in the story-critic (`.claude/agents/story-critic.md`): at the midpoint, a guess
but not certainty; after the cut, one sentence.

### What it suggests, and how to settle it
- **What it suggests.** At this length, the big narrated channels most likely do what the owner describes. The
  narrator says what the character said ("my mom said..."). Meanwhile the character acts on screen: turns, gestures,
  a talking head-bob. The big burned-in captions carry the words.
- **How sure that is.** This is a strong hypothesis from six caption reads, not a finding.
- **How to settle it.** Watch five on a phone, which takes about 3 minutes: Gneef #1 and #4, Bloopo #8 and #10, and
  Bloxolox #13. For each, note who speaks each line and how it appears on screen. Or rerun the research from a session
  with YouTube cookies set up.
- **For us.** It supports what STANDARDS 1.5 already says: narrator-first, other characters at most 1-3 lines of
  six words or fewer.
  - The four new scripts have at most one character line each, so each could also be said by the narrator ("Grandpa
    says they're our EYES") without losing a clue.
  - That makes each script work whichever way the owner decides.

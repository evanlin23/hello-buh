# How "closed society, deadly ritual, hidden truth about the outside" screenplays are structured (The Village, The Giver, Snowpiercer, City of Ember, The Hunger Games, The Maze Runner, The Truman Show, Divergent)

  - *Snowpiercer*: Bong Joon-ho and Kelly Masterson's "Final Draft 2011.11.11" (112 PDF pages), [Script Slug](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/snowpiercer-2013.pdf).
  - *The Hunger Games*: a Gary Ross / Suzanne Collins / Billy Ray draft (129 PDF pages, OCR'd and messy), [Script Slug](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-hunger-games-2012.pdf).
  - *The Maze Runner*: Noah Oppenheim's draft dated June 10, 2011 (110 PDF pages), [Script Slug](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-maze-runner-2014.pdf).
  - *The Village*: a fan transcript of the film, with action described, on [IMSDb](https://imsdb.com/scripts/Village,-The.html).
  - *The Giver* (2014), *Divergent* and *City of Ember*: dialogue transcripts of the films on Scripts.com ([Giver](https://www.scripts.com/script/the_giver_20316), [Divergent](https://www.scripts.com/script/divergent_7003), [City of Ember](https://www.scripts.com/script/city_of_ember_5610)).
- **How positions are given.**
  - For scripts: "pdf p.N/total (X%)". One script page is roughly one screen minute.
  - For transcripts: the share of dialogue lines, times the runtime Scripts.com lists (Giver 97 min, Divergent 139 min, City of Ember 90 min). These are approximate, give or take a few minutes, because dialogue isn't spread evenly.
  - "≈BL m:ss" converts a percentage to BUILD LIMIT's 60-minute clock (% × 0.6). Chapter boundaries fall at about 12, 24, 36 and 48 minutes, which is 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%.
- **Drafts are not the release films.** Where a draft differs from what was released, these notes say so: the Truman shooting script, the Snowpiercer 2011 draft with its girl voice-over, the Hunger Games draft that reaps Peeta first, and the Maze Runner 2011 draft with "the Consortium" and no WCKD.

## Q1. Openings (first image, first line, how the rules are shown), act breaks, midpoints, "all is lost", and endings

### Takeaway
All eight films show their ritual or system by about 12% of the runtime. Seven do it as a public, rule-bound procedure (a census, a draw, a ceremony, a funeral). The Maze Runner instead drops a newcomer into the system cold. Most of them have the hero taken out of the old world, or commit to leaving it, between about 15 and 25%. The Village and City of Ember are the exceptions: they are driven by petitions and clues instead. The structural midpoint is usually a physical threshold into the arena or the next car, at 40 to 50%. The worst loss is a death or a horror the system did deliberately, at 65 to 77%. The full explanation comes late, at 67 to 95%. The films end on a literal exit or opening (a door in the sky, a train door onto snow, a wall climbed, a hole looking down on the old city), or on a jump.

### Inferences
- In these films the first image does one of three things. It shows the watching apparatus without explaining it (Truman's two-way mirror). It shows death being mourned inside the lie (The Village's funeral). Or it shows a public procedure of counting and sorting (Snowpiercer's head count, the Reaping, the Choosing). BUILD LIMIT's cold open is a procedure (the pat-down, the census question, the two buckets), which matches the Snowpiercer and Hunger Games type.
- Several films bookend a phrase or gesture. Truman's "good afternoon, good evening and good night" opens and closes. Snowpiercer's draft runs "born on a moving train" against "born on the snow". Divergent's opening jump is echoed by "And then we'll jump." Ember's "fate ran another course" and The Giver's music echo work the same way. BUILD LIMIT's "forty-one" and the four-note song already belong to this family.
- The common percentages (Act 1 out at 15–25%, midpoint threshold at 40–50%, worst loss at 65–77%, final explanation at 85–95%) fall close to BUILD LIMIT's chapter boundaries of 20/40/60/80%. A five-chapter serial can borrow the shape almost directly.

### Gaps
- **Minute timestamps for the release films** (as opposed to drafts) were only found for The Hunger Games, via Save the Cat. Positions for Truman, Snowpiercer and Maze Runner are from scripts and may differ from the films.
- **The Truman Show's release opening and final lines** (Christof's "phony emotions" speech, the closing parking-guard exchange) could not be checked against a transcript; only the shooting script was read.
- **The Maze Runner's release-film rules speech** and the WCKD video dialogue were not found verbatim; the 2011 draft lacks both.
- **The Giver novel's exact opening sentence and final sentence** were not retrieved (the full text site returned 403), so the film's closing "echo" line can't be confirmed word for word against the book.
- **The Hunger Games draft's "ladies first" order.** The draft reaps Peeta before Prim; the release order wasn't confirmed from a transcript.

## Q2. How the hidden truth is revealed: how many steps, which discovery scenes, who explains it, and how the explainer scene is staged so it lands

### Takeaway
The best-regarded reveals arrive in four to seven escalating steps. Physical clues come first: an object, a message, a sound. A human witness follows: someone who was taken or got out. Then a forbidden archive or recording. The explainer comes last, and it is never a neutral lecture. It is staged as a talk show with a hostile caller (Truman), a steak dinner cross-cut with a massacre (Snowpiercer), a blind girl touching the monster costume (Village), or a video of a cheerful father killing a baby (Giver). Reveals that are a congratulatory speech or a recorded video at the very end (Maze Runner) are the ones critics call exposition.

### Cited Findings

**Truman Show: about seven steps, with the audience told mid-film and the hero told at the end**
- **Steps.**
  1. The SIRIUS light falls (pdf p.4).
  2. The dead father appears as a homeless man and is bundled away (p.26).
  3. Sylvia's warning in flashback (p.36–37).
  4. The radio scanner narrating his route (p.40).
  5. Escape attempts are blocked by staged obstacles: the travel agency, the bus, the bridge, the "nuclear" roadblock (p.54–65).
  6. A staged false fix, the father returned "with amnesia" (p.70–76).
  7. Christof's voice from the sky (p.106–108).

  ([Script Slug PDF](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-truman-show-1998.pdf))
- **The audience explainer (pdf p.74–83, 67–75%) is staged as a TV show inside the film**, "Tru Talk", "coming to you live from the Lunar Room on the 121st story of the OmniCam Ecosphere". The host notes "it is our practice to keep the image of Truman on screen at all times", so the hero stays on screen through the whole explanation. ([same](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-truman-show-1998.pdf))
  - **Exposition is turned into playback clips** of past "close calls": "Who can forget the infamous 'Christmas Present' incident in the seventh season?"; a parachutist with "TRUMAN, YOU'RE ON TV" on his chest; the seven-year-old Truman confronted at a bridge by "a savage DOG wearing a spiked collar".
  - **Christof explains the method in one line:** "Later, Kirk's drowning made much of this kind of intervention unnecessary." ([same](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-truman-show-1998.pdf))
  - **A hostile caller creates conflict inside the exposition.** "FEMALE CALLER 2: --He's not a performer. He's a prisoner." Christof answers: "He can leave at any time. If his was more than just a vague ambition… there's no way we could prevent him… ultimately Truman prefers the comfort of his 'cell'." The script then reveals "It is she who is the confrontational Caller": Sylvia. (pdf p.82–83) ([same](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-truman-show-1998.pdf))
  - **The thesis line:** "We accept the reality of the world with which we're presented." (pdf p.81) ([same](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/the-truman-show-1998.pdf))
- **The final explainer is short and is a temptation, not information.**
  - "TRUMAN: Who are you? CHRISTOF: I'm the creator. TRUMAN: The creator of what? CHRISTOF: A show… TRUMAN: A show. Then who am I? CHRISTOF: You're the star." … "Nothing was real." / "You were real. That's what made you so good to watch." … "You never had a camera in my head." … "there's no more truth out there than in the world I created for you… But in my world you have nothing to fear."

### Inferences
- **The working pattern for an explainer that lands:**
  - Put the hero's body at stake during the scene. Truman's image stays on screen; Snowpiercer cross-cuts to fights; the Village sends Ivy straight on a mission.
  - Have the explainer want something from the listener: Christof wants Truman to stay, Wilford wants Curtis to take his place, Edward wants forgiveness and a journey.
  - Let a hostile voice interrupt: Sylvia's call, Curtis's "Bullshit", Newt's "Proud?".
  - End on an image that is shown, not said: the static, Tim in the engine, the costumes.
- **A one-line question-and-answer volley lets a reveal pack many facts without a speech.** The Village does it ("The screams?" / "We created those sounds."). BUILD LIMIT's model scene (Ash and the cobblestone rig) and Wren's rule could borrow this rhythm: Buh asks, Ash answers in one line each.
- **A delivery device can be turned into a twist.** Snowpiercer's red letters, from the protein-block maker's "I don't write them" to Wilford's "I just wrote it. For you", show the device becoming one. BUILD LIMIT's allay deliveries (the compass, the name tag, "READY") are this kind of chain; the template is to have the last message's author or meaning flip.
- **The taken reappearing visibly** (Gerald's one-string violin, Tim in the engine) is the strongest "where they go" image in this set. BUILD LIMIT's thirty in the Below, and their climb out of the Needle in front of the city, are the same type of payoff.

- The Giver film's Chief Elder confrontation and the Giver's own long explanation scenes were not quoted in full; only the footage scene was read closely.

## Q3. How the ritual is made both public spectacle and private horror, and how characters talk about it (euphemisms)

### Takeaway
Every film films its ritual twice at once. The wide shot is ceremony: a crowd, a podium, a PA or emcee with a slogan, applause, a camera crew. The close shot is a single family's body language: a shirttail tucked, a hand held, a mother's "I love you... No matter what." The scene turns on the moment the private reaction breaks the public ritual: "I volunteer", a gasp when a son picks Erudite, "Pipeworks? Louder, so we can all hear." Euphemisms are cheerful, bureaucratic or taboo: "Released to Elsewhere", "Those We Don't Speak Of", "the bad color", "Happy Hunger Games", "Faction before blood", "Know your place".

## Q4. What critics and audiences called weak (plot holes, exposition dumps, flat endings), and how to avoid it

### Takeaway
The complaints cluster in four places:
- a twist that retroactively shrinks the stakes (The Village);
- a final act that stops for a monologue or a recorded explanation (Snowpiercer for some reviewers, The Maze Runner);
- an exposition-heavy opening, or a rushed last half-hour that "jump-starts" the sequel plot (Divergent, City of Ember);
- a mystery told to the audience at the start, so the characters' discovery has no suspense (City of Ember).

### Cited Findings
- **The Village.** Roger Ebert's review, as quoted by Far Out Magazine:
  - "a director of considerable skill who evokes stories out of moods, but this time, alas, he took the day off";
  - "colossal miscalculation";
  - "To call it an anticlimax would be an insult not only to climaxes but to prefixes";
  - "It's a crummy secret, about one step up the ladder of narrative originality from 'It was all a dream.'"
  - Far Out calls it a one-star review, aimed mainly at the twist. ([Far Out Magazine](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/the-2004-movie-so-bad-it-insulted-roger-ebert/))
  - Defenders exist, e.g. a Slashfilm "Unpopular Opinion" piece calling it one of Shyamalan's best. ([Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/the-village-defense/), title only)
- **Snowpiercer.**
  - Slate's Dana Stevens: "I felt a slight letdown in Snowpiercer's final scenes, when the mission's few survivors arrive at the engine room." She praised the forward motion: "As they progress forward car by car through the train—each transition marked by a bloody battle—". ([Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/movies/2014/06/snowpiercer_directed_by_bong_joon_ho_reviewed.html))
  - Search snippets report:
    - Deep Focus Review says the film "attempts to tie up several thematic and narrative strains within a few brief scenes at the end", but that the reveal means "everything about what came before is enhanced tenfold" ([Deep Focus Review](https://deepfocusreview.com/reviews/snowpiercer/));
    - Strange Horizons questions the "triumphant and literal smashing of the class system, which may have the side effect of ending the human race" ([Strange Horizons](https://strangehorizons.com/non-fiction/reviews/snowpiercer/));
    - Flick Filosopher calls the ending "the only honest and authentic 'happy ending'" ([Flick Filosopher](https://www.flickfilosopher.com/?p=42546)).
- **The Maze Runner.**
  - Brian Eggert, Deep Focus Review: the screenplay "delves into outright exposition and ends abruptly with all but flashing 'to be continued' on the screen"; "By design, the cliffhanger ending shows us images that allude to a larger, more complicated world outside of the Maze"; "diverting but, in the end, incomplete." ([Deep Focus Review](https://deepfocusreview.com/reviews/the-maze-runner/))
  - A search snippet also reports a reviewer praising how the film "gets all the necessary setup out of the way in a quick, natural introduction, free of voiceover or title-card exposition". The specific outlet could not be confirmed from the snippet, so treat this as unattributed.
  - Tor's review is headlined "'WCKD is Good,' But The Maze Runner is Bad". ([Tor](https://tor.com/2014/09/19/the-maze-runner-movie-review), title only)
- **Divergent.** James Berardinelli, Reelviews:
  - "An opening voiceover gives us the basics but a better understanding of the political structure might make the events" of the third act seem less sudden;
  - "the lengthy middle segment, when Tris trains to become Dauntless while hiding her Divergent nature, is absorbing cinema";
  - the film "suffers from a weak final half-hour where the need to jump-start the story" takes over;
  - "Divergent tells a complete story but it's evident it is intended as part of a greater whole".

  ([Reelviews](https://www.reelviews.net/reelviews/divergent))
- **City of Ember.** Brian Eggert, Deep Focus Review:
  - the third act "relies on a poorly rendered computer-animation boat ride over collapsing waterwheels";
  - "when you know the inevitable conclusion from the outset, that the people of Ember were always meant to resurface, the mystery is rendered pointless";
  - Bill Murray's "ignoramus Mayor".

  ([Deep Focus Review](https://www.deepfocusreview.com/reviews/city-of-ember/))
- **The Giver (2014).** Metacritic lists 8 positive, 20 mixed and 5 negative critic reviews. The Rotten Tomatoes consensus is that Noyce handles the visuals with grace, but the film doesn't dig deep enough into its source's ideas. ([Metacritic](https://legacy.prod.metacritic.com/movie/the-giver/critic-reviews); [Rotten Tomatoes](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_giver_2014); both via search snippet) Scripts.com's page lists the film at Rotten Tomatoes 35% and Metacritic 47. ([Scripts.com](https://www.scripts.com/script/the_giver_20316))

### Inferences (how a writer avoids each)
- **A twist that shrinks the world (Village).** Ebert's "crummy secret" complaint is about a reveal that makes past danger fake. The Village survives for its defenders partly because the reveal is followed by real danger: Noah in the costume, the wall, the elders' vote. A reveal should open a bigger, more dangerous question rather than close one. BUILD LIMIT's "the fog is not the void" reveal is followed by "Mom named Ash" and the Cradle rule, which keeps the stakes rising.
- **A terminal monologue (Snowpiercer, Maze Runner).** Split the explanation: part to the hero mid-film, part shown, part as a temptation at the end. Cross-cut the explainer with a live action thread whose outcome is uncertain, as Snowpiercer's draft does with the Namgoong and Yona fight. Never deliver it as a recorded video or a congratulations speech after the climax, as the Maze Runner does.
- **Front-loaded voice-over (Divergent, Giver, City of Ember).** All three open on narration listing the rules, and critics flagged the exposition. Truman, The Village and Snowpiercer show their rules through behaviour first: the lens, the buried flower, the head count. BUILD LIMIT's law chant is a diegetic ritual, which is the Ember oath / Divergent "Faction before blood" model and not a narrator. Kids' talk on the practice ledge is the Village and Truman model.
- **Knowing the outcome at the outset (City of Ember).** If the audience is shown the trick early, the dramatic question has to shift from "what is the secret?" to "will they get out?" and "who pays?". Truman does exactly this: the audience is in on it from minute one, and the suspense is escape and cost.
- **A rushed sequel-bait ending (Divergent, Maze Runner).** Both critics frame the endings as setting up the next entry. A serial can still end on a world-expanding button, but only after the chapter's own conflict is resolved on screen, as BUILD LIMIT's Chapter 5 sets out.

### Gaps
- No structural criticism of The Truman Show or The Hunger Games was retrieved in this pass; both are generally treated as successes in the sources read.
- RogerEbert.com returned 403 to direct fetches, so Ebert's own text on The Village (and his reviews of the others) was read only through Far Out Magazine's quotations.
- Audience-level complaints (forums, CinemaSins-style plot-hole lists) were not surveyed.

## Q5. Which structural moves map best onto a 5-chapter serial with a cliffhanger every ~12 minutes

### Takeaway
The eight films' natural turns fall close to 20/40/60/80%, so each BUILD LIMIT chapter break can be modelled on a specific proven beat:
- **Ch1 out (20%):** leave home or commit (the Capitol tunnel, Choosing plus the jump, "Receiver of Memory", the first gate breached).
- **Ch2 out (40%):** cross a threshold into the hidden space (the arena tube, the Yekaterina tunnel, the creatures in the village).
- **Ch3 out (60%):** a personal truth or betrayal (the Gilliam-type reveal, the release footage at 66%).
- **Ch4 out (80%):** the worst loss, with a ticking clock (Rue's death and the rule change at 69–75%; Curtis's confession and Wilford at 77–84%).
- **Ch5:** the public exit witnessed by a crowd, then an image of a wider world.

([same](https://savethecat.com/beat-sheets/the-hunger-games-bookmovie-beat-sheet-comparison))
- **Snowpiercer's car-by-car structure** ("each transition marked by a bloody battle") is a ready-made episodic engine. ([Script Slug PDF](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/snowpiercer-2013.pdf))
- **Snowpiercer's Act 1 break uses a suspended clue as a cliffhanger.** The capsule is fumbled, rolls away and is read only as the gates open: "Edgar grabs the rolling capsule and tries to open it… EDGAR drops the Red Letter: 'No Bullets.'" (pdf p.29–30) ([same](https://assets.scriptslug.com/live/pdf/scripts/snowpiercer-2013.pdf))
- **The Truman Show's script alternates Truman's world with the control room and the viewers.** Its escape sequence (pdf p.88–105) cross-cuts Truman's boat, the CONTROL ROOM and VIEWERS nearly every page. - **The Village's reveal ladder (74% to Ivy, 89% to the audience, 90–97% the wall)** spreads across the last two-fifths, with the human cost (Lucius stabbed, 58%) placed just before the first reveal. ([IMSDb transcript](https://imsdb.com/scripts/Village,-The.html))
- **The Giver's turns:** selected Receiver at about 17%; "Boundary of Memory" at about 41%; release footage at about 66%; Gabriel to be released at about 74% ("so I'll have to Release him to Elsewhere"). ([Scripts.com transcript](https://www.scripts.com/script/the_giver_20316))

### Inferences (mapped to BUILD LIMIT's five chapters; examples only, not critique)
- **Chapter 1 (0–12 min, "You Fall Once"): open on a procedure, plant one physical anomaly, end on a message from someone presumed dead.**
  - Models: Snowpiercer's head count with a counting rebel; Truman's SIRIUS light at 3%; the Hunger Games draft's "POP! BLACKNESS, like a video feed shorting", which reveals the death was public TV.
  - Truman's homeless "father" sighting (23%), and the Village's one-line rule-and-taboo exchanges in class, are the closest analogues for a chapter-ending "a dead person is alive" hook.
- **Chapter 2 (12–24 min, "The Seventeenth Bucket"): a heist or archive scene, then a threshold.**
  - Models: The Giver's archive of books and the "Boundary of Memory" clue (41%); Ember's reconstructed Instructions (51%); the arena tube at the Hunger Games midpoint (47%).
  - Ending on the first sight of the hidden world is the Hunger Games midpoint move ("rises into the arena in a tube"), pulled earlier to 40%.
- **Chapter 3 (24–36 min, "The Below"): reunion with the taken, a working model of the trick, and a personal betrayal as the cliffhanger.**
  - Models: Gerald's one-string violin and Tim in the engine (the taken seen); the Village's one-line Q&A for the mechanism; Snowpiercer's "We, Gilliam and I. Our plan." as the template for a cliffhanger line that reassigns guilt to a trusted figure.
- **Chapter 4 (36–48 min, "One In, One Out"): the worst loss plus a clock.**
  - Models: Rue's death followed by the rule change (69% to 75%), which turns grief into a new plan; The Giver's footage at 66%, then "so I'll have to Release him to Elsewhere" at 74%, which turns a horror into a deadline; Curtis's confession (77%), staged as a told story with one gesture.
  - Ending on a name or document ("Named: Tilly") echoes the Village box's newspaper clipping ("Billionaire Walker Shot to Death") as an object that rewrites a character.
- **Chapter 5 (48–60 min, "Thirty-Three"): a public exit witnessed by the crowd, the villain's choice, then an image of a wider world.**
  - Models: Truman's control room, viewers and the door in the sky; the District 11 and District 12 crowds returning a gesture; the Village elders standing one by one; Snowpiercer's polar bear ("a figure comes into view") as proof the outside is alive; City of Ember looking down at the old city from outside; Divergent's "And then we'll jump" as a last line that re-frames the ritual.
- **Cliffhanger mechanics that recur and suit a 12-minute release:**
  - a written or delivered message read aloud at the last second (Snowpiercer's capsules; BUILD LIMIT's name tag);
  - a voice from someone presumed dead or absent (Christof's "Truman!" from the sky; Truman's father);
  - a single-line accusation that reassigns guilt ("We, Gilliam and I."; "That's death.");
  - a hidden space opened by hand (the Village shed, Yona's floor hatch, the Box ceiling).
